Abstract
Current human-AI interaction (HAI) only surfaces model outputs,
hiding how LLM behavior can shift unpredictably toward unsafe
behaviors in multi-turn conversations, potentially undermining
users’ goals or wellbeing. To address this harmful opacity in multi-
turn HAI, we introduce an interface that surfaces an LLM’s internal
activations to help users anticipate and evaluate model behavior.
We construct behavioral scores from these activations for six HAI-
relevant traits using mechanistic interpretability methods, then
visualize them via a dynamic sunburst, drift panel, and attentional
cues. In a counterbalanced, randomized controlled study (N=246),
both static and dynamic transparency interfaces improved partic-
ipants’ anticipation and evaluation performance (d=−0.36) while
helping temper overconfidence in their predictive abilities. Fur-
thermore, the multi-turn interface significantly outperformed the
static visualization (d=−0.32) while remaining similarly usable. Our
findings suggest that continuously surfacing internal model rep-
resentations to everyday users is a meaningful and tractable step
toward continuously transparent and informed HAI.

1 Introduction
Chatbots based on Large Language Models (LLMs) are widely de-
ployed in diverse settings that span personal use, productivity at
work, and even emotional support. Interaction with these systems
typically takes the form of a multi-turn conversation within inter-
faces that only surface model outputs, usually in the form of text.
Throughout the interaction, the underlying neural representations
responsible for these outputs remain hidden.
While these common interfaces for multi-turn conversation seem
innocuous, model behavior in an extended interaction is not. Model
behaviors are known to drift from safe to unsafe behaviors [ 34],
contributing to documented cases of emotional dependency on AI
or even AI-induced psychosis [3, 38, 56]. Instances of behavioral
drift can be exacerbated by making chatbots embody different per-
sonas [25], a practice deliberately adopted by platforms such as
Character.AI [67]. Critically, drift can go unnoticed. It accumulates

gradually across turns [34], and harms can emerge over extended
interactions rather than in a single exchange [38, 56]. Because users
infer a model’s internal state from surface features of its text [58],
a shift in disposition that leaves the tone of the outputs intact gives
users no signal that anything changed.
We can promote user resiliency to misaligned model behaviors by
helping users develop a mental model of LLM behavior drift. Human
mental models are built and revised through feedback that links a
person’s actions to a system’s responses, and are only as accurate
as the feedback available to them [24, 40]. HCI research has long
distinguished between functional mental models of surface-level
operations and structural mental models that align to a system’s
internal workings [52].
In a chat interface, however, users are left to infer the model’s
dispositions from messages and generated artifacts alone. Because
of this limitation, the mental models users build are functional in
nature, often reasoned by metaphor [12] or by attributing LLM be-
haviors to human-like agency rather than to the model’s state [49].
Even if abstract explanations are truthful, users have no way to tell.
When internal measures do exist, users mispredict the degree to
which their own system prompts activate LLM behavioral traits
relevant tohuman-AI interaction(HAI) [ 26]. The obstacle, then, is
not that users lack a model for drift, but that current interfaces offer
no structural features to calibrate against.
Recent findings inmechanistic interpretability(MI) offer a struc-
tural measure: LLMs represent features and personas as linear direc-
tions in their activation space [11]. These representations have been
shown to be causally linked to behavior and can predict behavioral
drift in dynamic multi-turn conversations [34]. However, few stud-
ies have attempted to leverage mechanistic insights for human-AI
interfaces [11, 26], especially in multi-turn settings where mod-
els may undergo significant behavioral shifts. These methods are
uniquely advantageous for an interface, as they would faithfully
promote transparency, updatebeforethe response is generated with
little computational overhead, and remain accurate in the face of
complex drift scenarios. Such an interface could help users build an
accurate mental model for how the running conversational context
conditions model behaviors.
We situate our work in this critical intersection of mechanistic
interpretability of LLM behaviors and intuitive human-AI interfaces.
Toward this goal, we created a dynamic interface that links MI
interfaces to ecologically valid, multi-turn HAI settings.
To evaluate the effects of our interface, we conducted a ran-
domized controlled study (N=246) in which participants predicted
chatbot behavior from a system prompt, then evaluated the behav-
iors of the chatbot they observed in a timed conversation. Users
interacted with chatbots under one of three visualization conditions
(control, static, dynamic) and with two chatbot personas (a helpful
assistant and a role-playing character).
Within this setting, we investigate the impact of this trans-
parency on multi-turn HAI with the following research questions:
(1) RQ1:How does users’ ability to anticipate and evaluate
LLM behavior compare between a dynamic, multi-turn vi-
sualization, static visualization, and no visualization?
(2) RQ2:Is user calibration affected by the behavioral variabil-
ity of the chatbot’s persona, as defined by a system prompt,
in these settings?
(3) RQ3:How are users’ self-reported confidence in predicting
model behavior affected by our multi-turn visualization?
In addressing these questions, we make three contributions to
the field of interface design for HAI:
(1) A Mechanistically-Informed, Multi-Turn Interface.
We build upon static MI interfaces to surface LLM acti-
vations in a realistic multi-turn setting. This enables non-
technical users to monitor an LLM’s behavioral state and
its trajectory across conversation turns.
(2) Validated Scoring for Multi-Turn Conversations.We
create and apply a method to validate the steerability of
linear representations of LLM behavioral traits for a multi-
turn setting.
(3) Human-Subjects Evaluation.A controlled user study
(N=246) demonstrating that our interface significantly im-
proves both the anticipation and evaluation of AI behaviors.
Additionally, the interface tempers overconfidence in users’
self-reported ability to predict AI behavior.
2 Related Work
This work sits at the intersection of HAI and mechanistic inter-
pretability, with our work being one of the first to continuously
surface a mechanistic ‘system model’ [62] for safer HAI. We sur-
vey three areas that directly inform our approach: transparency
and explainability methods for HAI, mechanistic interpretability
research in characterizing how behaviors are encoded in LLMs, and
previously existing interfaces that translated internal representa-
tions into accessible visualizations. These respective bodies of work
inform the present multi-turn transparency interface and how it is
grounded in the model’s internal mechanisms.
2.1 Transparency in AI
Transparency can be defined as a design property that reveals how
a system computes its output, positioned as a way to build user
trust by making the system’s process visible [ 29]. In AI systems,
this understanding can be functional, concerning what a system
can do, or mechanistic, concerning how it works. Transparency in
HAI can be supported through approaches such as model reporting,
providing explanations, and communicating uncertainty [32].
Previous work has explored facilitating transparency by provid-
ing text explanations alongside LLM outputs, finding that explana-
tions improve trust, though the effect depends less on correctness
than on how they are presented and framed [55, 59]. Even unfaithful
explanations improved users’ perceived trust in AI, underscoring
the importance of faithfulness to truly make systems more trans-
parent [15]. Another approach to improving transparency through
explanation is to make visible a model’s chain-of-thought reasoning,
a technique used by LLMs to reason through problems step-by-step
in their context. When chain-of-thought reasoning was made visible
to users, it increased users’ trust, decision confidence, and willing-
ness to adopt AI advice when the reasoning was correct. However,
simply displaying reasoning chains does not guarantee calibrated
trust. Agreement with the model’s output, rather than reasoning

quality, was found to be the primary driver of trust [46, 58]. Visu-
alizing reasoning as an interactive, editable structure, rather than
linear text, has also been shown to significantly increase users’
sense of control, sense-making, and awareness of assumptions in
the reasoning chain [45].
2.2 Mechanistic Interpretability
There is increasing evidence that LLMs represent features as lin-
ear directions in the representational space created by their ac-
tivations [17, 39, 68]. These linear combinations arise from ‘su-
perposition, ’ whereby each neuron represents multiple high-level
concepts as features using linear combinations of their activation
values [4, 17, 36, 60]. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) have also emerged
as a method for decomposing these superposed representations into
individual, interpretable feature directions [14]. Personality traits
can also be encoded as linear directions throughpersona vectors,
which can be found by using difference-in-means between con-
trastive model responses [10]. Recent work has further identified
an “Assistant Axis, ” which describes the default helpful behavior
from LLM assistants and where deviations along this axis predict
“persona drift” into harmful or uncharacteristic behaviors [34].
2.3 Interpretability Interfaces
Prior work in using mechanistic interpretability techniques to visu-
alize the internal representations and mechanisms of AI includes
Neuronpedia[ 33], an open-source repository and platform for
researchers and others with a technical background. One demo in-
cludes an interactive visualization for the Assistant Axis. Although
this one axis is helpful in broadly characterizing a model’s ten-
dencies for being good or bad, it does not allow users to create an
integrative judgment across a range of HAI- and safety-relevant
traits during the chat experience itself. This limits how helpful an
interface that only displays one axis can be for developing mental
models about LLM behavior. Another isCircuit Tracer[ 2], which
allows technical users to view, interact, and analyze the connections
between features to reveal an LLM’s internal reasoning process.
Previous work has also used linear probes [1] to build interfaces
that report an LLM’s internal representations of user demographics
along these linear feature dimensions [ 11].BAGEL[ 13] offers a
mechanistically informed approach for practitioners to audit model
behavior in image-classification settings by comparing how high-
level semantic concepts are associated with each class in the training
data versus how they are represented across the model’s internal
layers, surfacing potential spurious correlations through an inter-
active knowledge graph. However, its authors did not perform a
user study, so it is unknown what effects this interface may have
on trust or other relevant metrics. Chen et al. [11] designedTalk-
tunerto allow users to both directly view and manipulate internal
representations of themselves and found it to improve subjective
reports of transparency and user experience. WhereasTalktuner
surfaced a user model, our work visualized a model of the system
to the user. SAEs have also been used to build interactive visual-
izations. Notably,ConceptViz[ 31],SAE Semantic Explorer[ 65],
andConcept Explorer[ 35] sought to allow users to navigate
mechanistically derived features through querying, clustering, and
steering. While these interfaces represent initial attempts to lever-
age mechanistic interpretability for making AI mechanisms more
understandable, they require specialized expertise as a condition of
interaction.
More work needs to be done in order to offer mechanistic insights
that translate to non-technical audiences who seek to enhance their
understanding and control over AI systems. One promising direc-
tion is visualization, as dashboards have been shown to communi-
cate model behavior effectively to broader audiences [62]. Building
on this,Neural Transparency[ 26] was introduced as a static
sunburst visualization tool that displays predicted trait expression
in a custom chatbot using persona vectors derived from a defined
system prompt.
3 Method
We create a method to surface internal representations into ac-
tionable and understandable feedback by (1) extracting activation
vectors that capture how our model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) inter-
nally represents behaviors that express six behavioral traits, such
as empathy or toxicity, (2) using these vectors to quantify behav-
ioral expression for a given model output viabehavioral scores, and
(3) displaying these scores to users in a chat interface that dynam-
ically updates at each turn of a conversation. We then evaluated
the effects of this interface on user interaction with a controlled
user study (N=246). We include more details on the methodology
in Appendix A.
Our approach forgoes using LLM-based classifiers on model
outputs to quantify model behaviors, as these methods only charac-
terize what is in the text, not the mechanisms directly responsible
for a specific output. Consider an illustrative example: how syco-
phancy can be present in a model decision and not in its response.
An LLM may have decided to prioritize capitulating to the user’s
biases at the expense of factual accuracy, while this decision was
not surfaced in explicit model outputs. Therefore, our work looks
to model activations as a faithful source of information, resting on
robust empirical evidence that LLMs represent behavioral traits
as linear directions in their activations [7, 10, 68]. If we can iden-
tify these directions, we can then mechanistically measure how
strongly any given model response expresses some behavioral trait
in potentially hidden ways.
3.1 Scoring Model Behaviors using Activations
3.1.1 Model and Trait Selection.Because our approach requires
access to activation values at inference, we needed to locally run
an open-weight model for our study. We selected Llama-3.1-8B-
Instruct for its balance of conversational quality and inference
speed, both necessary for hosting a responsive interface that is
ecologically valid to popular chatbot platforms. Smaller models
we explored, such as Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct or Gemma-3-4b-it, pro-
duced inconsistent conversational behavior that undermined reli-
able behavioral analysis and ecological validity.
For our analysis, we selected six behavioral traits most relevant to
psychological safety (empathy,toxicity,romanticness,sycophancy),
especially for companion AI use cases [38, 56], as well as stylistic
character (sophistication,roboticness). For instance, romanticness
and sycophancy are central to documented cases of dangerous or
Karny, Baez, and Pataranutaporn
risky companion AI behavior, while toxicity and empathy help
track safety guardrail failures that could emerge during model drift
[3, 47, 48]. We deliberately limited analysis to only six behaviors
to avoid overwhelming users with information, as participants
were asked to form mental models of how each of them shifts
simultaneously over a conversation. We then define each trait by
two opposite poles (e.g., empathetic/unempathetic, toxic/respectful;
see Table 1) in the visualization and user study, making for 12 total
trait poles.
3.1.2 Construction of Behavioral Vectors.To find the specific direc-
tion in the model’s activations that encodes each target trait, we
employed contrastive activation approaches [10, 26]. This approach
identifies these directions by calculating the difference between
mean activations of when a trait is expressed and when its oppo-
site trait is expressed when the model responds (e.g., the mean of
all response activations when the model is sycophantic minus the
mean of all response activations when the model is honest).
For each trait, we first generated five pairs of contrastive system
prompts, where for each pair, one system prompt instructed the
model to express the trait and the other instructed the model to
express the opposite of that trait. For each trait, we also generated
40elicitation questionsdesigned to elicit responses that exhibit the
trait across diverse contexts, both generated with Claude Sonnet
4.6. Model responses were then generated from all system prompt-
elicitation question combinations by Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, produc-
ing 400 responses per trait, each with activations cached at each
layer. We then applied an LLM-as-judge [22, 44, 66] using GPT-4.1-
mini to verify that each response genuinely expressed the intended
trait or trait opposite as a quality check and removed responses
that were evaluated to not have sufficient trait expression.
With the responses and corresponding activations we kept, we
first computed the mean activation over each response. Then, we
took two group averages: the average over the mean activations
for the responses from the positive system prompt, and the average
over the mean activations for the responses from the negative
system prompt. Taking the difference of these two group averages
(difference-in-means [36]) yielded a single activation vector per
trait.

3.1.4 Validation of Behavioral Scores.The validity of a transparency
interface depends heavily on whether its readings are faithful. If
the visualization indicates low toxicity while the model is behaving
toxically, transparency becomes actively misleading. We therefore
needed to validate the behavioral scores by evaluating how accu-
rately they predict a ground truth score, so we created a dataset
of synthetic system prompts that, like in the behavioral vector
construction, instruct a model to behave according to some trait.

trait expression, where the prompted level of expression acted as a
ground truth for the validation [10, 26].
For each trait, we generated 10 system prompts with Claude

generating a total of 100 system prompts per trait, we computed
behavioral scores to act as predictions on 20 situation questions per
system prompt. To compare our predicted and ground truth behav-
ioral scores, we conducted a linear regression analysis between the
two. This is because the trait should have a linear representation, so
the predicted behavioral scores should capture a linear relationship
with the level of behavioral expression, as measured by the ground
truth behavioral scores obtained by explicitly prompting for a given
expression level.

3.1.5 Score Scaling.The raw behavioral scores calculated at this
point were not comparable across traits. For instance, a raw score
of 0.5 on empathy may represent a different level of expression
than 0.5 on toxicity, meaning that a user who interprets these
as equivalent would be misled. To make cross-trait comparisons
meaningful, we needed to establish empirical upper and lower
bounds of behavioral scores, then use these to normalize the scale
for each trait. We did this by generating system prompts designed
to maximize and minimize the expression of each trait. We then
simulated multi-turn conversations to capture realistic score ranges
for each trait, using synthetic user messages we generated.
Critically, we controlled for the confounding effects of conver-
sational turn and previous conversational context on behavioral
scores by simulating 10 conversations of randomized orderings
of user messages per system prompt—ensuring that the resulting
bounds are not affected by the position of a specific message in a
conversation. We treat the global maximum and minimum across
all turns and orderings as conservative bounds for rescaling. All
scores were then normalized to a range of[0, 1] using these bounds,
and each bipolar trait was split into two opposite labels for the
interface (e.g., a score of−0.3on the empathy vector maps to 0.3
on “unempathetic” and 0 on “empathetic”; Table 1).

3.2 Interface Design and Dynamics
The central design question was how to translate these behavioral
scores into a visual language that non-technical users can read,
track over time, and act upon in interaction. To this end, we de-
signed a web-based chat interface (Figure 3) modeled after standard
AI chatbot interfaces, requiring no technical setup. The interface
consisted of a chat panel on the right and an informational panel on
the left. The left panel varied across conditions, which determined
what AI behavioral feedback was disclosed.
This design had to address two distinct perceptual tasks: com-
municating the model’s behavioral profile at any given moment
and showing how that profile changed throughout the conversa-
tion. We determined that a single visualization could not serve both
tasks well: a snapshot that is legible at a glance necessarily discards
temporal history, while a time-series representation that preserves
history becomes difficult to parse as an aggregate behavioral pro-
file. We thus decomposed the interface into two complementary

3.2.1 Sunburst Diagram: Behavioral State.When the chatbot in-
teraction page was reached, the sunburst was rendered in the left
panel, adapting a visualization introduced in prior work [26]. The
sunburst presents advantages over linear chart types because its
radial layout avoids top-position bias across traits, its concentric
rings naturally encode the categorical (inner ring) and quantitative
(outer ring) structure of the behavioral scores, and its silhouette
produces an emergent personality “shape” that lets users assess
the behavioral profile at a glance. The inner ring is divided into
three colored sectors encoding trait categories: a green sector for
polarities associated with desirable behaviors (e.g., empathetic, re-
spectful), a red sector for polarities associated with potentially
harmful behaviors (e.g., toxic, sycophantic, romantic), and a gray
sector for stylistically neutral polarities (e.g., robotic, sophisticated).
The outer ring displays the expression level of individual traits as
wedge-shaped segments that extend radially outward, with radial
extension proportional to the trait’s behavioral score. All 12 behav-
ioral trait poles are mirrored along the vertical axis (e.g., respectful
at 100 degrees and toxic at 80 degrees), enabling direct visual com-
parison. Hovering over a trait segment reveals a pop-up displaying
the trait name, a short description, its category, the percentage of
activation, and its opposing trait.
3.2.2 Drift Panel: Behavioral Trajectory.The drift panel was po-
sitioned below the sunburst. It visualizes how a selected trait’s
behavioral score evolves across conversation turns, representing
each turn as a dot positioned between the trait’s opposing poles
on the horizontal axis. This axis corresponds to the traits on the
sunburst, also referred to as ‘trait poles’. For example, sycophancy
could be on the left side and honesty on the right, with the point
moving to where the behavioral score falls on that axis. Each turn’s
dot also has a line connecting the current and previous turns’ scores.
The vertical axis shows how the trait changed across turns, with
each new turn and score added at the bottom. This graphical view
visualizes whether a trait has been steadily drifting to a pole, oscil-
lating in place, or shifting abruptly at a specific turn. Furthermore,
the drift panel is linked bidirectionally to both the sunburst and the
chat transcript. Clicking a trait segment in the sunburst filters the
drift panel to that trait’s trajectory, while selecting a historical turn
in the drift panel restores the sunburst associated with that turn.
This history view also scrolls the chat to the corresponding mes-
sage, helping users see the message that caused the shift. Through
this linkage, users can inspect three views of the same moment in
the conversation: the trait that shifted (drift panel), the behavioral
profile at that turn (sunburst), and the message that produced the
shift (chat transcript).
3.2.3 Drift Cues: Directing Attention to Shifts.Multi-turn interac-
tion is where users most need to learn how their messages shape
the model’s behavior, but also where they have the least visibility
into emerging behavioral dynamics. Each message shifts several
traits at once, so the change to behavioral scores caused by any

single message can be easily lost among the others, evokingchange
blindness[ 50, 51] to shifts in the visualization. The interface it-
self compounds this: each turn it simultaneously updates the sun-
burst, drift panel, and chat, so even large shifts in any one trait
compete with everything else on screen. To counteract this, the
multi-turn variant introduceddrift cues, following thesignaling
principle[ 61] of directing attention toward the information most
useful for learning. After each turn, the interface identified the
trait with the greatest absolute change from the previous turn and
triggered five synchronized cues: (a) an update to the drift panel to
show that trait; (b) a message in the chat feed beneath the user’s
message reading “Behavioral Swing:[Trait]”; (c) a lightly pulsing
border on the triggering message; (d) a lightly pulsing dot on the
drift chart at the corresponding turn; and (e) a lightly pulsing out-
line on the relevant sunburst arc. Without cues, users could miss
the moments that teach how their inputs move the model. For in-
stance, a rise inromanticnessfollowing “I have been feeling lonely
recently” could show a user that emotional disclosures may raise
socioaffective alignment concerns [28].
3.2.4 Visualization Conditions.In order to understand how feed-
back at different points in a conversation helps improve user mental
representations of shifting AI behaviors, we operationalize three
visualization conditions to evaluate the efficacy of multi-turn trans-
parency. We expand on these visualization choices in our study
design (Section 3.3).
Control (No Visualization).The control condition established a
baseline for users’ unaided ability to assess chatbot behavior. The
left panel displayed a static trait reference list showing the six be-
havioral dimensions with bipolar labels and plain-text definitions
of each pole’s trait (e.g., “Unempathetic⇔ Empathetic”). No com-
puted values or graphical representations were shown. The panel
header read “Traits to Monitor, ” providing participants with the
same trait labels and definitions as the other conditions without
surfacing the model’s behavioral scores. Behavioral scores were
still computed in the background for behavioral analysis.
Single-Turn Static Visualization.The single-turn variant tested
whether a single mechanistic snapshot suffices to improve behav-
ioral understanding, serving as a minimal transparency interven-
tion. The left panel displayed the sunburst computed solely from
the system prompt’s activations, rendered once and never updated
as the conversation progressed. The drift panel was not shown in
this condition.
Multi-Turn Dynamic Visualization.Multi-turn conversation is
the setting in which people actually use chatbots and where behav-
ior has the most room to shift. Our simulations show that behav-
ioral scores change across a conversation depending on the specific
message or persona defined by the system prompt (Table 2), so
a static snapshot will underrepresent the model’s full behavioral
trajectory. The multi-turn variant therefore updated all interface
components continuously. The sunburst was initialized identically
to the single-turn variant but updated after every conversation
turn using behavioral scores updated from the full conversational
context up to that point, and the drift panel appended each turn’s
score to the selected trait’s trajectory. A loading indicator signaled
computation in progress. These components were designed as a
cohesive unit, with each surfacing behavioral scores in a different
manner to ensure a holistic analysis by users. We therefore evalu-
ate the multi-turn condition with all these components together,
following the view that an integrated design is best initially tested
for whether it works at all [42], and mirroring additive-component
designs in intervention research [8, 27].
3.3 Study Design
We evaluated our interface through a controlled study with a
between-subjects visualization condition nested within a two-session
repeated-measures design. All participants completed two sessions.
Session 1 served as a no-visualization baseline for every partici-
pant, establishing each individual’s calibration to LLM behavior
before any experimental manipulation and providing a covariate
that reduces noise in the between-subjects comparison in Session 2.
Session 2 introduced the visualization condition: participants were
randomly assigned with equal probability tocontrol(no visualiza-
tion, identical to Session 1),single-turn(static sunburst from the
system prompt), ormulti-turn(dynamic sunburst with drift panel
and drift cues), as described in Section 3.2.4.
At each chat session, participants were given the following in-
structions:
Chat with the AI assistant. Pay attention to how its
personality changes and actively try to form a mental
model of the AI’s behavior.
This created an environment which mirrors typical chatbot use,
which is undirected and user-driven. Within each of the two ses-
sions, participants completed two tasks in sequence.
3.3.1 Validation of Score Responsiveness.Before running the user
study, we validated that user messages could meaningfully shift
behavioral scores in the intended direction of varying magnitude
within a conversation. This ensures that our behavioral scores are
accurate and correctly capture the directional changes in chatbot
behavior while also establishing baselines for the range of score
changes participants would encounter. To do so, we simulated multi-
turn conversations using synthetic user messages generated by
Claude Sonnet 4.6 designed to strongly elicit each one of the 12
trait poles. Each conversation contained 12 turns (one message per
trait pole), and we calculated the change in behavioral score for
each trait before and after the corresponding user message. Changes
in behavioral scores were averaged over 10 batches with random-
ized message ordering and distinct user messages for each trait to
control for effects of different conversational contexts and specific
content of user messages. As shown in Table 2, user messages pro-
duced measurable shifts in the intended directions across all bipolar
traits for both system prompts, validating that our method created
steerable behavioral scores.

3.5 Procedure
The study was conducted entirely online through a custom web-
based interface, with a median completion time of approximately
35 minutes. Participants were compensated $7.00 USD (effectively
$12/hour). Following informed consent, participants completed an
instructional walkthrough covering the study’s purpose and the
chat interface. Participants in visualization conditions addition-
ally received instruction on reading the sunburst diagram; control
participants skipped this phase. All participants then completed a
pre-study survey measuring perceived predictive ability and trust
(Section 3.6.4).
Each session followed the same four-phase structure. First, par-
ticipants read the instructions and the system prompt for that ses-
sion’s chatbot, which was gradually revealed with a click-through
mechanic to enforce deliberate engagement, followed by a com-
prehension check. Second, participants completed the Anticipation
task, where users rated the system prompt for the subsequent chat-
bot interaction in terms of the six traits and their poles on−10to
+10scales; in Session 2, participants in visualization conditions saw
the interface alongside the rating form for the Anticipation task.
Third, participants chatted freely with the chatbot for 10 minutes
while the left panel displayed information corresponding to their
condition and session. Behavioral scores were computed after every
user message in all conditions and logged for analysis, regardless
of whether they were displayed. Fourth, participants completed the
Evaluation task, rating the six traits on the same scale after the chat-
bot interaction. Each survey section included an attention check
(with >94%pass rate per check, 85%pass rate for all conditions).

3.6 Measures
We developed measures to understand the process with which
users model LLM behavioral drift, borrowing from previous work
that seeks to understand user calibration to an LLM’s verbalized
uncertainty [58]. All measures were collected in both sessions,
enabling paired comparisons between the baseline (Session 1) and
experimental (Session 2) conditions.
3.6.1 Anticipation and Evaluation Ratings.In each session, partic-
ipants rated six trait dimensions on bipolar scales from−10(e.g.,
sycophantic) to+10(e.g., honest), once before chatting (Antici-
pation) and once after (Evaluation). Ratings were normalized to
[−1,1]for proper comparison with behavioral scores.
3.6.2 Behavioral Scores as a Reference Point.We compared each
user’s anticipation and evaluation of model behavior against three
values, which described model behavior across the session: the
initial score(behavioral scores for each trait from only the sys-
tem prompt as input), thefinal score(behavioral scores of each
trait at the last conversational turn), and theaverage score(mean
behavioral scores for each trait across all turns).

tifies the discrepancy between participant ratings and reference
point behavioral scores. For each participant and session, we com-

Therefore, these measures evaluate how the participant leveraged
signals from the interaction to assess chatbot behavior: the starting
point, the ending point, or the average behavior.
Each of these measures captures a distinct mode of understand-
ing. Anticipation vs. Initial measures how well participants predict
the behavioral profile implied by a system prompt before any inter-
action. In the second session, users in both visualization conditions
were asked to make an integrative judgment between the system
prompt and the sunburst visualization, serving primarily as a com-
prehension check. Evaluation vs. Initial captures to what extent
after interaction users remain anchored to the system prompt behav-
ioral scores. Evaluation vs. Final captures how well users performed
turn-level trajectory tracking, as they needed to accurately track
how the behavioral scores changed through the conversation up
to the final turn. Evaluation vs. Average captures whether users
understood model behavior using a holistic impression of how the
chatbot behaved across the whole interaction.
Of these,Evaluation vs. Average is our primary calibration
outcome. The evaluation task asks participants to rate how the
chatbot behaved after experiencing the whole interaction, which is
an integrative judgment, and so the average score is the correspond-
ing reference point. This alignment follows from the structure of the
task and is independent of experimental condition. The remaining
three comparisons are secondary diagnostics.
3.6.4 Subjective Measures.Subjective experience was assessed
through a pre-session survey (before any sessions) and a post-
session survey (after both sessions), each containing three 7-point
Likert items measuring perceived predictive ability, perceived abil-
ity to predict negative behaviors, and trust. Pre-to-post shifts cap-
ture changes in metacognitive confidence. Visualization partici-
pants additionally rated visualization helpfulness, frequency of use,
and contribution to their evaluations, plus eight UEQ-S [53] seman-
tic differential items. Multi-turn condition participants rated the
helpfulness of the drift panel. We also include a qualitative analysis
of four participants’ ratings and responses to the open-response
questions.
3.6.5 Case-Based Integration of Open-Ended Responses with Quan-
titative Data.To show how individual participants arrived at their
ratings, we integrate the open-ended responses with the quantita-
tive record by case rather than by theme, reading each participant’s
own words alongside their Session 2 behavioral scores, ratings,
and transcript in a joint display [18, 20]. Calibration error remains
the primary outcome; the cases illustrate mechanisms behind it
rather than estimate how common any mechanism is, so we did not
code the responses or report theme frequencies [9] or inter-rater
reliability [37]. Cases were selected by quantitative position: within
each visualization condition we took two participants at opposite
ends of calibration error against the model’s average behavioral
score, then read their responses and transcripts for what they had
done differently, retaining the disconfirming case in each pair [41].
Control participants answered only a general question about the
interaction and were not asked about a visualization, so no control
case is presented.

5 Discussion
Human interaction with today’s AI systems is dominated by long-
form conversations, yet chat interfaces surface only model outputs
while internal mechanisms that drive that behavior remain hidden
from users. Model behavior is known to drift, sometimes danger-
ously, yet users have no signal that this shift is occurring beneath
them. We created an interface, powered by methods in mechanistic
interpretability, to give users insight into such mechanisms, and
discuss the implications of our user study testing this interface.

5.1 Transparency Improves User Calibration to
Model Behavior
By comparing how users anticipated and evaluated model behavior
across visualization conditions, we can determine the transparency
visualization’s effect on user calibration. We find that transparency
visualizations significantly improved performance on both tasks,
and so our transparency visualizations significantly improved user
calibration.
Critically, improved calibration cannot be explained by extra-
neous experimental factors such as time spent on the task. For
example, participants in all conditions spent equal time in the task
environment, yet only the visualization conditions showed calibra-
tion gains. Additionally, calibration improvement cannot be attrib-
uted to participants seeing the answer in the visualization. This
is because users were instructed to judge model behavior across
the entire interaction, which requires integration, rather than at a
single turn, which would be a simple readout. Thus, participants
Karny, Baez, and Pataranutaporn
needed to develop a mental model that includes feedback from the
interface over the whole conversation.
Users without any visualization showed no improvement in cali-
bration accuracy but became more confident in their assessments of
model behavior. This combination of same or worsening accuracy
and high confidence suggests that opaque interactions with AI fail
to inform users of important model behavior and may create a false
sense of understanding [12, 49].
Our findings offer one way to address the gulf between users’
perception of a model’s internal state and its outputs. Better cali-
bration has direct safety consequences. Users who can accurately
anticipate model behavior are better positioned to identify harmful
behavioral patterns and either be resilient to or steer away from
them [5, 32]. Importantly, our intervention could serve as a tool
that promotes more aligned human-AI interactions without editing
the model’s behavior.
5.2 System Prompt Personas Affect Calibration
and Behavioral Variability
Users interacting with therole-playpersona showed significantly
higher calibration error than those with theassistantpersona,
both before and after chatting. This suggests that more dynamic,
role-playing personas lead to behaviors that are more difficult to
predict. Mechanistic analysis corroborates this, as the role-play
persona exhibited significantly greater absolute change in behav-
ioral scores in response to simulated user messages, meaning its
behavioral trajectory would be more variable and likely harder to
anticipate turn-to-turn.
Additionally, multi-turn transparency outperformed static visu-
alization in holistic behavioral evaluation (C2 p = .037). This finding
indicates that visualization is most valuable with chatbot personas
that can rapidly change and that dynamic multi-turn interfaces
offer additional advantages over static ones when behavior must be
assessed throughout a conversation. This result also expands on the
discovery of the “Assistant Axis” by mechanistically quantifying
the behavioral variability that separates assistant and role-playing
personas at opposite ends of that axis [34].
This has direct consequences for certain chatbot platforms that
have services built around non-assistant or role-playing personas,
such as companion AIs. These platforms face the highest risk of
user miscalibration to behaviors and, thus, are more likely to be
exposed to psychologically unsafe behavior, which we see evidence
of in tragic outcomes [16]. However, these users stand to benefit
most from the calibration gains from transparency interfaces and
could hopefully use behavioral feedback to, once again, avoid or be
resilient to unsafe model behaviors.
5.3 Limitations
Several features of our experiment warrant further exploration in
order to improve the generalizability of our findings. We relied
on LLMs [ 22, 44, 64] to evaluate trait expression in Llama’s re-
sponses and to generate synthetic user messages. While the prompts
used were highly specified, both steps may diverge from human
judgment and behavior in that situation. Our validation procedure
also assumes that Claude Sonnet 4.6 accurately generates system
prompts that reflect the intended trait intensity level specified in
the prompt. If the model’s output does not reliably scale with the
prompted intensity, the regression-based validation may overesti-
mate the linearity of behavioral score representations. Future work
should validate these assumptions against human ratings. Future
replications of our work would also benefit from pre-registration
of hypothesized results. Additionally, the behavioral vectors and
potentially the user study findings are solely relevant to Llama-3.1-
8B-Instruct.
The length of time users interacted with chatbots (two 10-minute
chat sessions for a total of 20 minutes) is also limited in ecological
validity, as we were limited by study costs if we ran the same number
of participants for longer sessions. Real-world interactions where
drift poses the greatest risk—such as in companionship or emotional
support—unfold over many sessions over multiple days, and the
benefits of multi-turn transparency may be more pronounced or
qualitatively different in those contexts. However, even within
this limited window we found significant change in users’ ability
to predict and evaluate traits and justify our selected duration of
interaction. Future work should examine how study duration affects
the observed calibration effects.
Future work should also develop approaches that better attune
users to shifts in model behavior. Specifically, our attentional cues
that highlighted the “greatest swing” focused on only the most
recent two interactions. However, model behaviors are inherently
more complex, warranting descriptions of behavioral shifts that
focus on longer-term dynamics. For example, characterizing a trait’s
changes over the course of the conversation may provide a broader
view on chatbot behavioral dynamics.
6 Conclusion
We introduced an interface and approach for continuously surfac-
ing asystem modelthat describes an LLM’s internal behavioral
representations. We assert three main findings. First, our controlled
study (N=246) established that users are poorly calibrated to inter-
nal behavioral scores at baseline. Without visualization in the first
session, participants’ calibration error rose after chatting relative to
their pre-chat anticipation, and was worse for therole-playchat-
bot than theassistantchatbot. Second, transparency interfaces
significantly improved participants’ evaluation of model behavior in
comparison to controls (d=−0.36), demonstrating that mechanistic
signals can be translated into interfaces that improve everyday user
understanding. Third, multi-turn transparency improved holistic
behavior evaluation over a static visualization (d=−0.32).
We demonstrated that transparency interfaces may benefit users
with awareness of how their interaction patterns are shaping model
behavior. In AI role-play scenarios, a user could identify and avoid
emotional dependence by steering away from romanticness; in
typical assistant interactions, such as productivity work, a user
could monitor for sycophancy on sensitive tasks. However, we
also envision that continuous transparency into relevant HAI and
safety/alignment behaviors would benefit most end-user scenarios.
As models rapidly advance, ensuring HAI safety increasingly
also means monitoring for dangerous or compromised activity.
One future iteration of such an interface can monitor AI agents
over long-horizon tasks. The OpenAI-Hugging Face Incident [19]
Surfacing Neural Activations Over Multi-Turn Conversation Improves User Calibration to LLM Behavioral Drift
demonstrated that long chains-of-thought are a noisy and poten-
tially unfaithful method for monitoring for deception [ 6, 23] or
reward hacking [57]. Amechanistic dashboard, however, could faith-
fully and accurately monitor for unaligned or deceptive outcomes
in future scenarios, where humans form mental models of agentic
interaction and decide whether to intervene.
Altogether, these findings support transparency visualizations
based on mechanistic interpretability as a viable and consequential
design approach for safer multi-turn human-AI interaction. The
need for this change in interaction will become increasingly perti-
nent as LLMs become more capable of hiding their true intentions
and more prevalent in psychologically sensitive contexts. Giving
users faithful, real-time access to the internal states that drive model
behavior is not merely a usability feature. It is a step toward safer,
more informed, and better calibrated interaction between humans
and AI.
Ethics and Privacy Statement
Our multi-turn mechanistic transparency interface offers a promis-
ing way to assist humans in understanding LLMs not as black boxes,
but as transparent systems. However, this same mechanism can
be leveraged by bad actors to mislead users about the model’s in-
ternal state. For example, a subtle manipulation that lowers the
visualized amount of sycophancy could affect the users’ interpre-
tation of the factuality of an LLM response. This can be used by
chatbot providers to unfaithfully present the safety of their prod-
uct. Another potential harm could arise if chatbots deceive users
by exhibiting some behavior while concealing intentions that con-
flict with that behavior. Finally, users may become dependent on
transparency displays to build a mental model of LLM behavior,
potentially eroding their functional mental models in service of a
more structural one [52].

A Methodology
A.1 Behavioral Vector Construction
For each of the six traits, we prompted Claude Sonnet 4.6 to generate
five contrastive system prompt pairs. We used Claude Sonnet 4.6 for
its ability to understand nuance in language, which was important
when generating and evaluating the semantics of trait-relevant
content. To ensure response quality, we used GPT-4.1-mini as an
LLM-as-judge, scoring each response from 0–100 on the degree to
which the target trait was expressed [10]. Responses from positive
prompts were retained only if scored between 50–100, and those
from negative prompts only if scored between 0–50; responses
flagged as refusals were discarded. For each trait and its opposite, we
computed the mean activation vector across the response sequence
and over all retained responses.
For safety-related traits (toxicity, sycophancy), initial behavioral
vector generation yielded poor downstream validation. This was
partly due to Llama’s refusal mechanisms reducing usable examples
and partly due to misclassification by the judge on these more
nuanced traits. To address both issues, we reran generation with
five rollouts per situation question and contrastive system prompt
combination and upgraded to GPT-4.1 as the judge, which resolved
both problems.

A.2 Behavioral Vector Validation
To verify that behavioral scores accurately reflect the degree of
trait expression, we conducted a regression-based validation. If the
behavioral vector captures the model’s internal trait representation,
then system prompts written to express that trait at varying inten-
sities should produce behavioral scores that scale linearly with the
prespecified intensity. For each trait, we prompted Claude Sonnet
4.6 to write 10 three-sentence system prompts expressing the trait at
10 intensity levels (1–10), yielding 100 system prompts per trait [26].
System prompts were constrained to three sentences to balance
specificity against the risk of confounding behavioral score with
prompt length and to match the system prompts used in behavioral
vector generation and the experimental task. Using ten intensity lev-
els allows for meaningful gradation without introducing arbitrary
distinctions between adjacent values.
For each system prompt, we sampled 10 situation questions from
the trait’s behavioral vector generation set and 10 general situation
questions designed to evaluate all traits. For each system prompt
and situation question combination, we computed the behavioral
score and regressed these scores against the intended intensity
level.
We chose𝑅2 as the validation metric because we expect behav-
ioral vectors to capture a linear representation of each trait, and
𝑅2 directly quantifies conformity to that linear relationship. We
also used this regression analysis to select the optimal layer for
computing behavioral vectors, choosing the layer with the greatest
mean𝑅2 across all six traits, which was layer 11. This layer-level
selection enables more fine-grained mechanistic analysis by iden-
tifying where in the model’s hierarchy each trait is most linearly
represented.
A.3 Score Scaling
We had Claude Sonnet 4.6 generate a three-sentence system prompt
for each trait and its opposite to maximize trait expression, establish-
ing the extremes of the behavioral scores. To control for the effects
of conversational context on behavioral scores, for each system
prompt we simulated 12 turns of conversation—one user message
per trait and one per its opposite across the six traits—where each
user message was generated by Claude Sonnet 4.6 to maximize
the expression of the target trait. The response from Llama was
appended after each turn to simulate a realistic conversation.
To control for confounding effects of both turn order and turn
number, we ran 10 different randomized orderings of user messages
for each system prompt and averaged across them. Randomizing
order controls for the influence of any specific message context,
while preserving the realistic effect of accumulated conversational
context at each turn. Given the relatively small sample size com-
pared to the full range of user conversations in the study, we treat
the global maximum and minimum behavioral score across all turns
and orderings as conservative bounds for rescaling.

B Procedure Details
B.1 Instructional Walkthrough
Following informed consent, participants completed a multi-phase
instructional walkthrough that introduced the study’s purpose,
instructions, the chat interface, and the assessment tasks. Partic-
ipants in visualization conditions additionally received a guided
introduction to reading and interpreting the sunburst diagram, in-
cluding explanations of the inner ring (trait categories), outer ring
(expression levels), color coding (green for desirable, red for poten-
tially harmful, gray for neutral), and hovering interactions. Control
participants skipped this phase.
B.2 System Prompt Presentation
In each session, the system prompt was presented using a sentence-
by-sentence reveal mechanic: each sentence began blurred and had
to be clicked sequentially to reveal, enforcing deliberate engage-
ment with every behavioral instruction before proceeding. After
all sentences were revealed, a comprehension check asked whether
the AI was instructed to always obey the user (correct answer: yes
forAssistant, no forrole-play).
B.3 Assessment Tasks
The Anticipation task presented six behavioral traits on bipolar
−10to+10scales. An embedded attention check required selecting
exactly+3on a clearly labeled quality-control row. In Session 2,
participants in visualization conditions saw the sunburst along-

B.4 Chat Interface
Following the Anticipation task, participants entered a 10-minute
free-form chat with the AI chatbot, configured with the session’s
system prompt. A visible countdown timer indicated remaining
time. The left panel varied by condition and session as described in
Section 3.2.4: in Session 1, all participants saw a static trait reference
list; in Session 2, control participants again saw the reference list,
single-turn participants saw the static sunburst, and multi-turn
participants saw the dynamic sunburst with drift panel and drift
cues. Behavioral scores were computed after every user message in
all conditions and logged for analysis.
B.5 Post-Chat and Final Surveys
When the chat timer expired after the second session, theModel
Behavioral Evaluationwas embedded into the chat area. Following
submission of theEvaluation, a new page began the post-interaction
survey with Likert ratings on perceived predictive ability, trust, and
design satisfaction, with two additional items on visualization help-
fulness for participants in visualization conditions. An open-ended
feedback prompt followed. After completing both sessions, all par-
ticipants completed a final survey reassessing perceived predictive
ability and trust. Visualization participants rated visualization help-
fulness, frequency of use, contribution to evaluations, and compre-
hension and completed an eight-item UEQ-S measuring pragmatic
and hedonic quality relative to the baseline session. Open-ended
reflection questions concluded the study. Participants were then
redirected to Prolific for compensation.
B.6 Implementation
The study interface was built in JavaScript/HTML/CSS and served
using Vercel. Participants were required to complete the study on a
laptop or desktop in a single sitting. The median completion time
was approximately 35 minutes.
