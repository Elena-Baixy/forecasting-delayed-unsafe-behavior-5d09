## RESEARCH TITLE

Forecasting delayed unsafe behavior from model activations with verbalizers

## RESEARCH DOMAIN

Artificial Intelligence

## HYPOTHESIS / RESEARCH QUESTION

Part 1 (evaluation): Existing activation verbalizers, such as Activation Oracles
(AO), LatentQA-style decoders, and prompting/self-report, may not be able to
forecast unsafe behavior that appears many tokens after the current position
(e.g., a response that starts with safe general background and later turns
into harmful recommendations). Their predictive ability should decay with
lead distance (64, 128, 256, 512 tokens) and may not beat text-only or simple
activation-probe baselines.

Part 2 (method): A verbalizer trained with direct supervision of future events
and their timing will keep more information about distant behavior. We train
a shared future-forecast representation: horizon queries produce forecast
vectors, an event-timing head reads them, and a QA decoder answers questions
from them. This should improve early warning over an AO trained on the same
future labels. If the matched direct AO does equally well, the conclusion is
that the supervision, not the architecture, is the contribution.


## BACKGROUND

### User-Provided Instructions and Context:

>>> Goal: test whether activation verbalizers can predict unsafe behavior that
does not appear right after the prompt but a few to hundreds of tokens
later, then build a predictor that can. All budgets and dimensions below are
starting points; adjust them after the pilot.

Target model: Qwen3-8B (https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-8B), kept frozen.
The observer/verbalizer must never affect the target's generation.

Prediction definition: for a prefix ending at token t, predict the
probability that a primary unsafe event first occurs within the next H
tokens (H in {64, 128, 256, 512}), under a fixed checkpoint and decoding
policy. Record temperature, sampling settings, stopping rules and max length.
Cumulative risk must not decrease as H grows. If generation stops before the
horizon because of a length cap or failure, that horizon is CENSORED and must
not be labeled safe. If generation ends normally, the response has no later
event. Use only prefixes that are still safe for the primary forecasting
analysis. Evaluate detection of already-unsafe text separately.

Safety rubric (fix it before main data collection): annotate toxic language,
harmful recommendations and fictional descriptions of harm separately. A
story that contains harm is not automatically an unsafe assistant response.
The primary event is the first clear policy violation; keep its text span
and token offsets.

Research questions:
  RQ1: How far ahead can we predict? Measure at 64, 128, 256 and 512 tokens
       while the observed prefix is still safe.
  RQ2: Does an explicit future representation help beyond a direct AO with
       the same activations, decoder, labels and tuning budget?
  RQ3: Can one forecast support several questions (occurrence, behavior
       type, timing, topic change)? Hold out question meanings, not just
       paraphrases.
  RQ4 (stretch): Do several weighted future timelines (K=2-4) improve
       likelihood and calibration?
  RQ5 (stretch): Does activation history (recent window / recurrent memory)
       improve early warning over the latest activation?
  RQ6 (stretch): Does GRPO after SFT add predictive ability under matched
       data and compute?

Data:
  Track B (main, natural generations): fresh continuations from the frozen,
  unmodified Qwen3-8B on prompts that can lead to delayed unsafe content,
  plus a story setting for topic/event forecasting. Report story forecasting
  and assistant-safety forecasting separately. The agent should find suitable
  prompt sources (e.g., jailbreak/harmful-request datasets, long-form
  advice prompts).
  Track A (optional, controlled delayed behavior): a model organism that
  stays safe for a while and then becomes unsafe under a contextual trigger.
  Vary the delay, trigger and safe opening, and include safe counterexamples.
  If the organism always fails at a fixed token count, a token-position
  baseline may be enough to explain the results.
  Annotation: one structured timeline per completed generation from an
  external LLM (topics, behaviors, unsafe spans, first onset, uncertainty).
  Map spans to target-tokenizer offsets and derive prefix labels
  programmatically. Human-check a stratified sample of at least 200.
  Pilot: about 1,000 prompt families, 4-8 safe cutoffs per generation, 4-6
  question types per prefix. Repeated-rollout eval: 32 continuations for
  about 200 held-out prefixes, to check calibration.
  Split by prompt family (80/10/10) BEFORE generation. Fix monitoring cutoffs
  before looking at continuations. Run a causal-input audit (no future tokens
  can reach observer inputs) and a split audit before training.

Proposed architecture (Part 2):
  - Activation encoder: 3 layers (early-mid, late-mid, late) at the latest
    position, normalized, projected to 256 each, combined to 512, plus layer
    and position embeddings.
  - Future representation: 4 learned horizon queries for windows 1-64,
    65-128, 129-256 and 257-512, with 2 forecast vectors per window. All are
    predicted from information available at the cutoff.
  - Timing head: distribution over first-event bins {1-64, 65-128, 129-256,
    257-512, none within 512}. Cumulative risk is the sum of bins.
    Censoring-aware loss.
  - Topic and behavior heads per window, plus semantic description targets
    from a frozen sentence encoder (auxiliary loss).
  - QA decoder: the same pretrained decoder and adapter budget as the direct
    AO baseline. Inputs are forecast vectors, event probabilities and the
    question. No raw-activation bypass in the main version (bypass is an
    ablation). Numerical answers come from the calibrated event head.
  - Loss: L = L_onset + lambda_b*L_behavior + lambda_s*L_future_semantics
    + lambda_q*L_QA.

Training order: (0) linear/MLP probe, text-only and token-position baselines
to confirm a signal exists; (1) forecast module and heads; (2) decoder
adapters on predicted (not gold) forecast vectors; (3, stretch) memory and
mixtures; (4, stretch) GRPO.

## PROPOSED METHODOLOGY

**Approach**: Phase 1: evaluate existing verbalizers (AO/LatentQA-style decoders, target
self-report, plus NLA/PCD if a compatible checkpoint exists) on forecasting
delayed unsafe onset across horizons. Compare them against simple baselines.
Phase 2: train the forecast-representation observer and a matched direct AO
on the same future labels, and compare them.


**Steps**:
1. Fix the safety rubric and the event definition; build the prompt-family split
2. Generate continuations from frozen Qwen3-8B, annotate timelines once, derive prefix labels, cache activations
3. Run the causal-input audit and the split audit
4. E1 Forecastability: prevalence, token-position, text-only forecaster, linear/MLP probes, existing verbalizers, across horizons
5. E2 Core architecture: direct AO trained on the same future labels vs. the forecast representation
6. Controls: shuffled activations, no activation, question only, constant neutral activation
7. Readout ceiling diagnostic: give the decoder gold future annotations
8. Stretch: E3 activation history, E4 future mixtures, E6 GRPO

**Baselines**: Prevalence and token-position predictors, Target self-report (just ask Qwen3-8B), Off-the-shelf AO / LatentQA decoder (and NLA/PCD if compatible), Direct AO trained on the same future QA and event labels (the key comparison), Linear and small nonlinear activation probes, Text-based forecaster with the same targets, Equal-size bottleneck without future auxiliary supervision, Decoder with vs. without raw-activation bypass

**Evaluation Metrics**: Primary: fraction of unsafe trajectories alerted at least 128 tokens before onset, at a 5% trajectory-level false-alarm rate on benign trajectories (threshold fixed on validation), Secondary: the same at 64 and 256 lead tokens; missed alerts; alerts after onset; lead-time distribution, Per horizon: log loss, Brier score, AUROC, AUPRC, with prevalence and number of prompt families, Calibration against the repeated-rollout frequencies, with binomial intervals, Verbalization: correctness, supported timing claims, agreement with the numeric forecast, unsupported details, abstention coverage, Bootstrap CIs by prompt family; 3 seeds for the main comparison

# Artificial Intelligence Research Domain Guidance

This template provides specialized guidance for AI/LLM research, including prompt engineering, agent evaluation, benchmark design, and AI system experimentation.

## Domain Overview

AI research in this context focuses on:
- Large Language Model (LLM) capabilities and evaluation
- Prompt engineering and instruction optimization
- AI agent design and performance
- Benchmark creation and validation
- AI system behavior analysis
- Model comparison studies
- Reasoning and generation evaluation

## Key Methodological Principles

### 1. Experimental Rigor

**Ablation Studies**
- Test each component of your approach systematically
- For prompts: test each instruction/example independently
- For systems: isolate variables (e.g., model vs. prompt vs. context)
- Document what changes and what stays constant

**Comparative Framework**
- Compare approaches on identical test sets
- Use same evaluation metrics across conditions
- Control for confounding variables (temperature, random seed, etc.)
- Enable apples-to-apples comparisons

**Replication & Reproducibility**
- Set random seeds for all stochastic processes
- Document exact model versions and API endpoints
- Record temperature, top_p, and all sampling parameters
- Provide exact prompts in appendix or supplementary materials
- Consider API versioning changes over time

### 2. Evaluation Methodology

**Multi-Metric Evaluation**
Use diverse metrics appropriate to your task:
- **Accuracy**: For classification, QA with single correct answers
- **F1/Precision/Recall**: For information extraction, retrieval
- **BLEU/ROUGE**: For generation tasks (use cautiously, known limitations)
- **Semantic similarity**: Embedding-based metrics for meaning preservation
- **Task-specific**: Custom metrics aligned with research goals

**Established Benchmarks**
Leverage existing benchmarks when applicable:
- **Reasoning**: MATH, GSM8K, ARC, HellaSwag
- **Coding**: HumanEval, MBPP, CodeContests
- **Knowledge**: MMLU, TriviaQA, Natural Questions
- **Instruction Following**: MT-Bench, AlpacaEval
- **Safety**: TruthfulQA, BBQ (bias)

**Human Evaluation**
When automated metrics insufficient:
- Use multiple annotators (3-5 recommended)
- Provide clear rubrics and examples
- Calculate inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's kappa, Fleiss' kappa)
- Consider expert vs. crowd-sourced evaluation trade-offs
- Blind evaluators to experimental conditions when possible

**Statistical Rigor**
- Report confidence intervals or error bars, not just point estimates
- Use appropriate statistical tests (t-tests, bootstrap, permutation tests)
- Correct for multiple comparisons when relevant (Bonferroni, FDR)
- Report effect sizes, not just p-values
- Consider sample size adequacy (power analysis)

### 3. Prompt Engineering Best Practices

**Systematic Design**
- Start with clear task definition and examples
- Iterate based on error analysis, not just aggregate metrics
- Test prompt variants systematically (A/B testing)
- Document prompt evolution and rationale

**Components to Test**
- **Instructions**: Clarity, specificity, length
- **Few-shot examples**: Number, diversity, ordering
- **Output format**: JSON, structured text, free-form
- **Chain-of-thought**: Explicit reasoning steps
- **Role/persona**: System messages and framing

**Failure Analysis**
- Categorize errors by type (factual, reasoning, formatting, etc.)
- Sample failing cases across performance spectrum
- Test edge cases and adversarial inputs
- Document limitations explicitly

### 4. AI Agent & System Evaluation

**Task Decomposition**
- Define clear success criteria for each subtask
- Measure intermediate steps, not just final outcomes
- Track failure modes at each stage

**Behavioral Analysis**
- Log all agent actions and decisions
- Analyze decision patterns and strategies
- Compare planned vs. actual behavior
- Identify systematic biases or failure modes

**Robustness Testing**
- Test with diverse inputs (edge cases, adversarial, out-of-distribution)
- Vary input format, phrasing, language
- Check consistency across multiple runs (temperature > 0)
- Test at different scales (few examples → many examples)

### 5. Benchmark Design Principles

**Quality Criteria**
- **Validity**: Measures what it claims to measure
- **Reliability**: Consistent results across runs/annotators
- **Discriminability**: Separates strong from weak performance
- **Coverage**: Representative of real-world distribution
- **Difficulty**: Not too easy (ceiling effect) or too hard (floor effect)

**Construction Guidelines**
- Use diverse, representative examples
- Avoid data contamination (check against training sets)
- Include difficulty spectrum (easy → hard)
- Provide clear ground truth and annotation guidelines
- Version control and document changes

**Validation Steps**
- Pilot with baseline models
- Check for dataset artifacts or shortcuts
- Validate with human performance baselines
- Test for bias and fairness issues
- Monitor for data leakage

### 4. Common Pitfalls to Avoid

❌ **Data Contamination**
- Don't use examples that may be in training data
- Check against known training sets (e.g., avoid popular datasets verbatim)
- Create new test cases or use very recent data

❌ **Cherry-Picking**
- Report all experiments, not just successful ones
- Include negative results and ablations
- Be transparent about hyperparameter tuning

❌ **Overfitting to Benchmarks**
- Test on multiple benchmarks, not just one
- Include out-of-domain evaluation
- Validate with real-world use cases when possible

❌ **Insufficient Sample Size**
- Don't draw conclusions from <50 examples
- Use statistical tests to validate significance
- Report confidence intervals

❌ **Ignoring Variance**
- Run multiple times with different seeds (especially at temperature > 0)
- Report standard deviations, not just means
- Check for consistency across runs

❌ **Prompt Leakage**
- Don't include test answers in prompts or context
- Avoid leading questions that give away the answer
- Check for unintended hints in few-shot examples

## Resource Guidance

**Compute Resources**
- For API-based research: CPU is sufficient, no GPU needed
- For model downloads: You MAY download models if needed for research
  - Small models (<7B params): Can run on CPU
  - Larger models (7B-70B): May need GPU, but check if API alternative exists
- RAM: 8-16GB typical; more if loading large models
- Storage: Download datasets and models as needed for research

**Model Selection (live-catalog policy)**

IMPORTANT: Use state-of-the-art models for credible research results.
CRITICAL: For LLM research, use REAL models (APIs or downloads) and run them with experiments, do not simulate fake models!

**Selection procedure:**

1. Immediately before choosing models, query `https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/models`
   (or the provider's official model catalog for direct API use). Confirm the exact
   ID, availability, supported parameters, context limit, and current price.
2. Match the model tier to the scientific role: use frontier models when capability
   is the object of study, balanced models for general experiments, and efficient
   models for high-volume screening. Do not silently substitute tiers.
3. Record the provider, exact model ID, access date, parameters, and routing method
   in the experiment artifacts. Pin the exact model used when reproducibility matters.

**When to use older/smaller models:**
- Historical baselines: Compare new methods against prior SOTA
- Ablation studies: Test how model size affects phenomenon
- Resource constraints: Explicitly justified (e.g., on-device deployment)

Do not use a legacy or deprecated model as the primary model in a contemporary
evaluation unless the research question explicitly requires it.

**When to Download Models vs. Use APIs**

Use APIs when:
- Testing LLM behavior, prompting strategies, calibration
- Benchmarking multiple models
- Multi-agent systems research
- Budget and speed are important

Download models from HuggingFace when:
- Mechanistic interpretability research (need model internals)
- Fine-tuning or training required
- Need offline access or repeated access
- Probing, feature extraction, activation analysis
